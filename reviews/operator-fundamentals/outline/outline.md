# Peaking Power Plant Operator Fundamentals

An adult with no power, electrical, mechanical, industrial, or market background, preparing to begin supervised, site-specific operator training at a US natural-gas peaking plant.

This course does not qualify anyone to operate a plant, and it does not authorize hazardous work. Approved facility procedures and site authorization govern every real action.

Most lessons stay general to US gas peakers and say where designs differ. Viper Power, six GE Frame 7B simple-cycle peakers in ERCOT, is a factual worked example when a plant is named.

54 lessons. Lesson time 4232 minutes (70 hours 32 minutes). Final assessment 60 minutes. Total 4292 minutes (71 hours 32 minutes).

Each lesson is planned between 60 and 90 minutes, with short sections, two checkpoints, and a 10-question knowledge check. Videos listed here are candidate topics only.

### Lesson 7 — How fluids move (85 min)

Prerequisites: Lesson 4.

Objectives:

- Explain that a fluid moves from higher pressure toward lower pressure when a path is open.
- Describe a pump at the level of what it does, not how to overhaul it.
- Say why flow and pressure are different indications.

Key topics: Fluid flow; Pressure difference; Pumps; Flow as a separate quantity.

Component plan:

- **pump.**
  - What it is: A pump is a machine that adds energy to a liquid so the liquid can move through a pipe.
  - Scientific principle: The pump raises the pressure of the liquid. Flow still requires an open path from the discharge toward a lower pressure.
  - How it works: The driver turns the pump. The pump takes liquid in at the suction and sends it out at the discharge. If the suction path or the discharge path is blocked, the intended flow does not happen.
  - Main components: A suction connection, the pumping element, a discharge connection, and a driver. Seals and bearings are part of the machine and are not overhauled in this course.
  - Operating cycle: Stopped, then running and moving liquid, then stopped again. This lesson does not assign a start procedure.
  - Interactions: The pump depends on a source of liquid, a driver, and open piping. A valve later in the path can stop the flow the pump is trying to make.
  - Why the operator needs it: The operator needs the pump because cooling water, oil, and other liquids do not move themselves to the places the unit needs them.
  - What goes wrong: A failure mechanism is a loss of the pressure rise, or a driver that is not turning the pump. Observable symptoms are no flow, a discharge pressure that is not established, unusual noise, or leakage at the pump. Report the symptom. Do not invent a repair.

Sections:

- What a pump does to a fluid — 14 min (core)
- Checkpoint — flow is not pressure — 4 min (checkpoint)
- What goes wrong with a pump, and the symptoms you can observe — 10 min (core)
- Visual — pump, pipe, and valve — 7 min (visual)
- Example — oil and cooling water as fluids, designs vary — 8 min (example)
- Key terms — 5 min (terms)
- Knowledge check — 10 questions — 12 min (quiz)
- Summary — 4 min (summary)

Example: Cooling water and lube oil are named as plant fluids that have to be moved. The lesson does not specify a pump type for every peaker.

Key terms: Fluid, Flow, Pump, Valve, Pressure difference.

Diagram: A pump, a pipe, and a valve. Flow direction, a pressure rise at the pump, and a valve that can block the path.

### Lesson 8 — Valves, and what they do to a path (81 min)

Prerequisites: Lesson 7.

Objectives:

- Describe a valve as a device that can open, throttle, or close a flow path.
- Separate valve position from the flow and the pressure the valve is acting on.
- Name beginner-level failure mechanisms and the symptoms an operator can observe, without a repair step.

Key topics: Valves; Open, throttled, and closed; Position versus flow; Failure mechanisms and observable symptoms.

Component plan:

- **valve.**
  - What it is: A valve is a device that opens, throttles, or closes a flow path.
  - Scientific principle: Fluid moves only where a path is open and a pressure difference exists. The valve changes the path. It does not, by itself, create the pressure difference.
  - How it works: A stem, actuator, or handwheel moves an internal element. Open leaves a path. Closed blocks it. A position between the two restricts the path. Some valves are built to move to a defined position if their power source is lost. That position is a design fact, not a number this course assigns.
  - Main components: A body in the pipe, an internal element that moves, and a means of moving it: handwheel, motor, air, or oil. Position indication may be separate from the valve.
  - Operating cycle: Open, moving, and closed are the positions an operator talks about. The site procedure decides which position is required. This lesson does not line valves up.
  - Interactions: A valve works with the pump or the pressure source on that pipe. Closing the valve is not the same action as stopping the pump. Instrument air or control oil may be what moves the valve.
  - Why the operator needs it: The operator needs the valve because the plant has to be able to admit or stop a fluid without taking the whole system apart.
  - What goes wrong: A failure mechanism is an element that does not move, or a valve that moves to the wrong position for the condition. Observable symptoms are a position that does not change when asked, a flow or pressure that does not match the claimed position, or leakage at the body. The symptom is reported. The repair is not taught here.

Sections:

- A path is only useful if it can be opened and closed — 5 min (intro)
- What a valve is and how it changes a path — 11 min (core)
- Checkpoint — position is not flow — 4 min (checkpoint)
- How a valve works with the pump and the pipe — 13 min (core)
- Checkpoint — stopping the pump is a different action — 4 min (checkpoint)
- What goes wrong with a valve, and the symptoms you can observe — 9 min (core)
- Visual — three valve positions — 7 min (visual)
- Example — a cooling or oil valve, type not assigned — 7 min (example)
- Key terms — 5 min (terms)
- Knowledge check — 10 questions — 12 min (quiz)
- Summary — 4 min (summary)

Example: A valve in a cooling-water or oil line, drawn as a class of device. The lesson does not assign a valve type to every peaker.

Key terms: Valve, Position, Throttle, Fail position.

Diagram: A valve in three positions. Open, partly open, and closed, with flow marked only on the open path. No stroke times.

Video candidate: A public clip of a valve opening and closing a visible flow path. Watch for: What changes in the path when the valve moves, and what would be different if it did not move.

### Lesson 20 — How a generator makes alternating current (88 min)

### Lesson 21 — Transformers, and why voltage is raised (89 min)

Objectives:

Component plan:

- **transformer.**
  - What it is: A transformer transfers alternating-current power from one voltage to another through a magnetic field.
  - Scientific principle: A changing current in one winding produces a changing flux, which induces a voltage in the other winding. Direct current does not keep that flux changing, so a transformer is an alternating-current device.

  - What goes wrong: A failure mechanism is damaged insulation, a winding fault, or a cooling problem that lets heat build. Observable symptoms are unusual noise, heat, a smell, leakage of cooling fluid, or a protective trip. Report the symptom. Do not approach the equipment or invent a limit.

### Lesson 22 — Breakers, protection, synchronization, and a trip (87 min)

Objectives:

### Lesson 23 — Air in, and why compression heats it (85 min)

Prerequisites: Lesson 4, Lesson 5, Lesson 7.

Objectives:

- Trace inlet air into the compressor.

- Name filtration as the reason the inlet exists.

Key topics: Inlet air; Filtration; Compressor; Pressure rise; Temperature rise.

Component plan:

- **compressor.**
  - What it is: On a gas turbine, the compressor is the section that draws inlet air in and raises its pressure.
  - Scientific principle: Work done on a gas raises its pressure, and the temperature rises with that compression. The lesson states the result without a gas-law derivation.
  - How it works: Inlet air passes a filter and enters the compressor. Rotating blades, driven by the shaft, push the air to a higher pressure and a higher temperature. The compressed air then goes to the combustor. Stage counts and pressure ratios are not stated.

  - What goes wrong: A failure mechanism is blockage at the inlet, damage to the blades, or a stall condition the design is built to avoid. Observable symptoms are unusual noise, unusual vibration, a pressure or temperature reading that does not match the expected direction of change, or a trip. No pressure limit is given. The symptom is reported.

- **gas-turbine.**

### Lesson 28 — Lube oil, cooling, and control oil (90 min)

Prerequisites: Lesson 5, Lesson 8, Lesson 26.

Objectives:

- Explain why bearings need a film of oil.
- Separate cooling from lubrication.
- Describe hydraulic and control oil as the fluid that moves some devices, and note that the layout varies.

Key topics: Bearings; Lube oil; Cooling; Hydraulic oil; Control oil.

Component plan:

- **pump.**
  - What it is: In the lube and cooling systems, pumps are the machines that move oil and cooling fluid.
  - Scientific principle: The same pressure-rise principle as the fluid lesson. Oil is pumped so a film can reach the bearings. Cooling fluid is pumped so heat can be carried to a cooler.
  - How it works: A lube pump takes oil from a reservoir and sends it to the bearings. A cooling pump moves the cooling fluid through the cooler and back. Layouts differ. This lesson does not count the pumps on a named unit.
  - Main components: Reservoir or source, pump, piping, and the bearings or the cooler the fluid serves. Filters and coolers are supporting pieces.
  - Operating cycle: A pump may need to be running before the shaft is allowed to turn, and it may need to keep running while the shaft coasts down. The site procedure sets that order. No duration is given here.
  - Interactions: Bearings depend on the lube pump. The cooler depends on flow. A valve in the oil or cooling line can block what the pump is doing. The gas turbine cannot be treated as ready if these loops are not.
  - Why the operator needs it: The operator needs these pumps because a turning shaft without oil, or a hot fluid that is not being cooled, is a machine at risk.
  - What goes wrong: A failure mechanism is loss of the oil film or loss of cooling flow. Observable symptoms are low oil pressure, a rising temperature, leakage, unusual pump noise, or a trip associated with those systems. No pressure or temperature limit is stated. Recognize the symptom and report it.
- **valve.**
  - What it is: In the oil systems, valves direct or stop lube oil, cooling flow, and control oil.
  - Scientific principle: The valve still only changes the path. Control oil is a fluid used to move devices. It is not the same job as the oil film in a bearing.

- **compressor.**

  - What goes wrong: A failure mechanism is loss of air, a stuck actuator, or a positioner that does not follow the command.
